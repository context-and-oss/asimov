# Steering

*A **Development** discipline, decided at the end of Scoping.*

## Purpose

Establish how the project will be governed and then govern it: the weekly progress meeting where scope, time and budget are reviewed, the monthly steering committee where the bigger decisions are made, and the monthly plan review that keeps the plan and the risk picture aligned.

Steering is the layer above the delivery ceremonies. It answers a different question from the sprint: not *what are we building* but *are we still doing the right thing, at an acceptable cost, on an acceptable timeline*.

## Why it matters

Without it, nobody is watching the whole. The delivery rhythm optimises the next two weeks and is very good at it — which is precisely why it cannot notice that the project has been quietly going the wrong way for three months.

The steering committee exists so the customer can make internal corrections. Sponsors who hear about a problem in month five had five months in which they could have solved it. Keeping them informed at a high level, monthly, is not reporting overhead — it is giving them the chance to help.

## How it is done

**Decide how the project will be managed after Scoping**, with the customer's project manager rather than to them. Start from the standard structure and adapt to the customer's reality. Our own team must know what is expected of them for the project to be managed at all — which is why this connects directly to onboarding the team.

**Run three meetings.**

| Meeting | Frequency | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Progress meeting** | Weekly | Review scope and changes, time spent, and risks. Coordination for the coming period |
| **Plan review** | Monthly | Look at remaining work from above: dependencies, challenges, risks. Output is an updated plan *and* an updated risk picture |
| **Steering committee** | Monthly | The bigger decisions, and risks escalated to where they can be helped. Sponsors present |

**Bring decisions, not updates.** A steering committee that receives a status report and asks no questions is a meeting nobody needed. Bring the things that require a decision, and what happens if it is not made.

**Escalate deliberately.** A risk goes to the steering committee when it needs help that the project cannot give itself. Escalating everything trains people to ignore it; escalating nothing wastes the forum.

## What you can do at each level

| Level | What you can do |
|---|---|
| **Do it** | You can run a progress meeting from an agenda, and prepare steering material from the project's own data |
| **Show it** | You can chair a steering committee so it produces decisions, and judge what belongs in it versus what the project should resolve itself |
| **Build it** | You can design the governance for an engagement — what forums, who sits in them, what each decides — and adapt it when the engagement changes shape |
| **Own it** | You can steer a project in difficulty through its sponsors: present a hard picture, get the decision you need, and keep the confidence of the room |

## How to get better

**Internally**

- The old delivery model's **Management** page and its project handbook, adapted per engagement.
- The **cadence and meeting structure** discipline, which places these three meetings in the wider rhythm.
- The **status reporting** discipline — steering runs on what reporting produces.

**Externally**

- Scott Berkun, *Making Things Happen* — practical on project governance and on the politics of steering without authority.
- Patrick Lencioni, *Death by Meeting* — specifically the argument that strategic and tactical meetings must be separate, which is the whole reason steering exists apart from the progress meeting.
- **PRINCE2's project board** concept — heavier than we would use, but a clear model of what a governing body is accountable for and what it delegates.

## Common failure

**Governance by status.** The committee is briefed and decides nothing, so decisions drift back to the project, which cannot make them.

**Escalating everything.** The forum becomes a queue and stops being useful.

**No sponsor present.** The one person who could unblock things is represented by someone who cannot.

**Dropping it when busy.** The months where steering is skipped are exactly the months it existed for.

**Steering the delivery instead of the project.** A committee discussing sprint content has replaced the team rather than governed it.

## Artefacts

The agreed governance structure, part of the project description · progress meeting notes · steering committee material · the updated plan and risk picture from the monthly review.

## How it varies by approach

Scrum keeps steering strictly separate from the ceremonies — the failure mode is letting the ceremonies absorb it. Kanban steers through the service delivery review. Use-case delivery uses the quarterly business demo as its sponsor-level forum, where the conversation is value delivered rather than work done. Business Continuity reduces to a periodic service review.

**Traditional vs agentic.** The content shifts. Less time is spent on whether the build is on track, and more on whether the customer's decisions are keeping pace and whether what is being built is still the right thing. That is a better use of a steering committee — and it means the questions brought to it should change too.
