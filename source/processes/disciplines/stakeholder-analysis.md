# Stakeholder analysis

## Purpose

Work out who has to be convinced for the work to succeed, what each of them cares about, and what happens if they are ignored. Not an org chart: a map of interests, influence and the gap between the two.

The delivery model asks for this early, because the answer changes what we propose, not just how we communicate.

## Why it matters

Solutions rarely fail on the build. They fail because the person who could have said "this will never work with how we invoice" was never in the room, or because the sponsor who funded it left and nobody had a second relationship.

Concretely, weak stakeholder analysis shows up as:

- A decision reopened in month four by someone who was never consulted.
- A solution that works and nobody uses, because the people who must change their day were treated as recipients.
- An escalation that surprises us, because we were only ever talking to one part of the organisation.
- A renewal lost to someone who had been building a relationship with the person we never met.

## How it is done

**Map before judging.** List everyone the work touches: who decides, who pays, who uses it, who operates it afterwards, who loses something if it succeeds. That last group is the one most often missed and most often decisive.

**For each, establish three things.** What do they want from this? What is their influence — can they stop it, slow it, or only complain about it? And what is their current stance: sponsor, supporter, neutral, sceptic, opponent?

**Then decide what to do about it.** A map with no actions is a diagram. Each significant stakeholder gets a name against them: who from our side holds that relationship, and what the next contact is.

**Revisit it.** People change roles, and a reorganisation can turn a sponsor into an absence. Re-run it at each phase gate and whenever the customer's organisation moves.

**Separate two analyses that are often confused.** Stakeholder analysis is about the people who decide and are affected. Analysing the *end users* — how they work, what they need, how adoption will land — belongs to Adoption. The old delivery model made this distinction explicitly and it is worth keeping.

## What you can do at each level

| Level | What you can do |
|---|---|
| **Do it** | You can produce a stakeholder map when asked — roles, obvious interests, who decides — and keep it updated when someone tells you it has changed |
| **Show it** | You can find the non-obvious stakeholder yourself, including the one who loses something. You read stance rather than title, and you turn the map into named actions with an owner against each |
| **Build it** | You can use the map to shape the proposal itself: sequencing, scope, who sits in which meeting. You build relationship coverage so no connection to the customer has a single point of failure |
| **Own it** | You can read an organisation well enough to anticipate where a decision will stall, and design the engagement so it does not. You can teach someone else to see what you see, which is the hard part |

## How to get better

**Internally**

- The **Customer analysis** material in the old delivery model — one of its better-written pages, covering stakeholders, maturity, finances and appetite for change.
- The **responsibility checklist**: stakeholder overview and management is a line on it, which tells you who is expected to hold this on an engagement.
- Read a live engagement's stakeholder map and compare it with what actually happened. The gap is the lesson.

**Externally**

- Peter Block, *Flawless Consulting* — written for exactly this position: an outsider who needs influence without authority. The chapters on contracting and resistance are the relevant ones.
- Allan Cohen and David Bradford, *Influence Without Authority* — practical, and directly about the situation where the person who can block you does not report to anyone you know.
- The **power/interest grid** (Mendelow) — a five-minute framework worth knowing properly rather than half-remembering.

## Common failure

**Treating the map as a deliverable.** It gets made in week one, filed, and never opened again. A stakeholder map that has not changed in six months is either a very stable organisation or an unread document.

**Confusing seniority with influence.** The person who can quietly kill the work is often two levels down and holds an operational veto nobody wrote down.

**Only mapping the friendly side.** Sceptics are the most useful entries on the map, because they tell you what the objection will be before it is public.

## Artefacts

A stakeholder map, held wherever the engagement is set up. It feeds the **Change readiness** assessment, the **Adoption** plan and the **Communication** side of status reporting. It is an input to the customer analysis produced in Scoping, not a substitute for it.

## How it varies by approach

Little, in method. It varies in **cadence**: a use-case delivery engagement that runs for years needs the map maintained continuously, while a fixed-price build can largely map once and check at the gates.

It matters most where the approach itself asks something of the customer. Kanban needs a prioritiser with real authority; use-case delivery needs a product owner who can stop a pilot. If the stakeholder map cannot name that person, the approach will not work regardless of how well it is run.
