# Outcome definition

## Purpose

Convert what the customer says they want into something specific enough to build towards and measurable enough to check afterwards. The output is not a requirement list — it is a statement of what will be *different* when the work has succeeded, and how anyone would know.

## Why it matters

This is the discipline that makes outcome-based pricing possible at all. If nobody can say what the outcome was, there is nothing to price against and nothing to point at when the invoice is questioned. Under hours-based work you can be vague and still be paid; the moment the conversation moves to value, vagueness becomes expensive.

It also protects the delivery. An undefined outcome cannot be finished — it can only be abandoned or endlessly extended, because there is no agreed point at which it is done.

And it is the input to the Business Value Ladder. Without a defined outcome the ladder has no top rung and becomes a list of features with ambition attached.

## How it is done

**Start from the problem, not the request.** *"Can you migrate our documents to SharePoint?"* is a solution someone already chose. The outcome sits behind it: find documents faster, stop losing knowledge when people leave, work in one version of the truth.

**Ask what it costs today.** The value-discovery conversation runs on the customer's own economics. What does this process cost now — in time, in errors, in people, in lost sales? A customer who has never counted usually finds the exercise more valuable than the answer.

**Name the outcome category.** Revenue, cost, compliance, or stakeholder satisfaction. Most engagements claim all four and mean one. Forcing the choice sharpens the conversation and makes the value ladder honest.

**Write success criteria that are actually measurable.** SMART, and tied to a business outcome rather than a delivery milestone. *"Adoption above 80% of the target group within three months of go-live"* is a criterion. *"The solution is delivered"* is a receipt.

**Agree who measures, and when.** The customer owns benefit realisation — we make it measurable. That split has to be explicit at the point the outcome is defined, or it becomes an argument later.

**Write it down where both sides can see it.** An outcome agreed only in conversation reverts to whatever each party remembers.

## What you can do at each level

| Level | What you can do |
|---|---|
| **Do it** | You can turn a stated request into a written outcome with success criteria, given the customer's input, and name which outcome category it belongs to |
| **Show it** | You can run the value-discovery conversation unaided: get to what the process costs today, and find the problem behind the request rather than accepting the first answer |
| **Build it** | You can define outcomes that survive contact with delivery — specific enough to settle a scope argument, robust enough to still make sense when the solution changes shape. You can tell a customer their stated goal is not the real one |
| **Own it** | You can hold an outcome conversation at executive level and tie it credibly to a price. You can coach someone through the discomfort of asking a customer what something costs them, which is where most people stop |

## How to get better

**Internally**

- The **teaching deck** *How we (should) deliver* — the Business Value Ladder in full, with the four outcome categories and the exercise that goes with it. Start here.
- The **Business Value Ladder** discipline, which is the instrument this discipline feeds.
- The FDE role description's value-discovery method: seven questions to learn a customer's economics before pricing.

**Externally**

- Josh Seiden, *Outcomes Over Output* — short, and the clearest statement of the difference between what you build and what changes.
- Gojko Adzic, *Impact Mapping* — the method for drawing the line from a business goal down to a deliverable, and for spotting when there isn't one.
- Teresa Torres, *Continuous Discovery Habits* — on keeping the outcome conversation running rather than doing it once at the start.

## Common failure

**The outcome that is a deliverable in disguise.** *"A working document management system"* names what we build, not what changes. If the sentence would still be true with nobody using the thing, it is not an outcome.

**Criteria nobody can measure without a project of their own.** If verifying the success criterion requires data the customer does not collect and will not start collecting, it is decoration.

**Defining it once and never returning.** Outcomes drift as understanding improves. Revisiting them is legitimate; discovering at handover that everyone had a different one is not.

**Accepting the first answer.** The first stated goal is usually the sponsor's summary of someone else's problem.

## Artefacts

Feeds the **Business Value Ladder** directly — the outcome is its top rung. Appears in **A100** as the business need, and in the business specification the FDE writes. Success criteria are referenced again at handover, when the question is whether the value arrived.

## How it varies by approach

**Discovery and use-case delivery** need it most: without a defined outcome per use case, the validation gate has nothing to test against and "did it work?" becomes a matter of opinion.

**Analysis and fixed price** need it earliest, because the price is set against it.

**Kanban** is the odd one: a stream of maintenance work often has no single outcome, and forcing one produces fiction. There the outcome is a service level, and that is a legitimate answer.

Under **agentic execution** the discipline gets more valuable, not less. When building is cheap, the expensive mistake is building the right thing badly defined — and the paradox holds: more upfront human thinking is what lets the agents do more.
