# Expectation matching

*A **cross discipline**. It has no phase of its own and no artefact of its own — it runs through all of them.*

## Purpose

Make sure that at any moment, the customer's understanding of what they are getting, when, and at what cost matches ours. Not agreement — *sameness of picture*. Two people can agree in a meeting and leave with different pictures, and every one of those gaps eventually surfaces as a complaint.

The old delivery model calls this the thing that permeates the whole approach, and then leaves its page blank. It is the most-cited and least-described discipline we have.

## Why it matters

It is the discipline the others exist to serve. Documentation, risk handling and status reporting are the three mechanisms of being in control — and being in control is only visible to a customer through matched expectations.

The evidence is in the old model's own claim, and it is worth repeating because it is counterintuitive: **a project can succeed even when it exceeds time, budget and scope, provided contact stayed close and honest throughout.** The inverse is also true and more common — a project delivered to specification is remembered as a failure because nobody was told what was happening.

Under agentic delivery this gets harder, not easier. When more is produced per week, more can drift per week, and the gap between pictures opens faster.

## How it is done

**Say the uncomfortable thing early.** The whole discipline reduces to this. Almost every expectation failure is a delay in saying something that was already known.

**Communicate more than feels necessary.** The old model is emphatic: *it is MUCH better to communicate more than less.* Weekly, on time, budget and scope — by email or a documented meeting — whether or not there is news.

**Close the loop in writing.** A conversation is where expectations are set; a written trace is where they stay set. Not bureaucracy — one paragraph, sent the same day.

**Restate what things are not.** A prototype is not a deliverable. A pilot is not a rollout. An estimate is not a price. Each of these needs saying every time it is shown, not once at the start.

**Check the picture rather than announce it.** *"Just so we are aligned — what do you expect to see at the end of this sprint?"* The answer is the measurement. If it differs from ours, the gap was there before the question.

**Make what we need from the customer explicit.** Each approach requires something: a prioritiser, a product owner, decisions within a number of days. Stating it in Scoping turns a later problem into a shared commitment.

## What you can do at each level

| Level | What you can do |
|---|---|
| **Do it** | You can send the weekly update reliably — time, budget, scope — and write down what was agreed after a conversation, the same day |
| **Show it** | You can notice when a customer's picture has drifted and correct it before it becomes a complaint. You say the uncomfortable thing while it is still small, which is the whole discipline in one habit |
| **Build it** | You can design an engagement so drift is hard: cadence, artefacts and demonstrations arranged so the two pictures get compared regularly rather than assumed. You make what we need from the customer explicit in Scoping |
| **Own it** | You can reset a relationship where expectations have already broken, and rebuild trust from an honest account of what went wrong. You can teach someone to raise things early rather than hope, which is mostly teaching them that it is safe to |

## How to get better

**Internally**

- The old delivery model's **project management** page — the "communicate more than less" material, and the claim that a project can succeed over budget if contact stayed close and honest. Short and worth reading properly.
- The **status reporting** discipline, which is this one's main mechanism, and the teaching deck's section on what a report must contain.
- The **Issue** log on any live engagement. Repeated questions are drift made visible.

**Externally**

- Douglas Stone, Bruce Patton and Sheila Heen, *Difficult Conversations* — the standard work, and directly about the thing this discipline keeps failing on: saying it early.
- Kerry Patterson et al., *Crucial Conversations* — more tactical, useful when the conversation is already tense.
- Peter Block, *Flawless Consulting* — the chapters on contracting, which is expectation matching before there is anything to expect.

## Common failure

**Waiting for certainty.** *"I'll tell them once I know how bad it is."* By then it is worse, and the delay is the part they remember.

**Reporting activity instead of position.** A list of what the team did is not the same as where the project stands against time, budget and scope.

**One channel, one person.** Expectations are matched with an organisation, not with a project manager. If only one person on their side has the picture, the picture leaves when they do.

**Assuming a demo does the job.** A demo shows what exists. It does not say what it cost, what it displaced, or what is now at risk.

## Artefacts

No artefact of its own — it runs through all of them. It is visible in the weekly status update, the progress meeting, the steering committee, the demo, and in any written confirmation after a decision. Where it fails, the evidence usually shows up first in the **Issue** log: questions logged repeatedly because nobody trusted the verbal answer.

## Where it is exercised

Being a cross discipline, it has no phase of its own. It runs from the first conversation in Proposal to the closure report, and it is the one discipline that never pauses — a week without it is a week of drift.

Its mechanisms differ by approach:

- **Scrum** has it built in — demo and progress meeting — provided the management cadence is kept alongside the ceremonies. Dropping the second is the classic way to lose it.
- **Kanban** replaces the promise with a Service Level Expectation, which only works if the customer has genuinely accepted that a probabilistic forecast is the commitment. That acceptance is expectation matching.
- **Use-case delivery** carries the largest risk, because there is no release to force the conversation. The quarterly business demo exists for exactly this.
- **Prototyping** demands the most and the most explicitly: a working prototype looks finished, and agentic execution makes it look more finished, faster. Here the discipline must be written down as well as spoken.
