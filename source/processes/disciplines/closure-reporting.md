# Closure reporting

## Purpose

Close an engagement on purpose rather than by fading out. Record what was delivered against what was promised, what the value looks like, what we learned, and what the customer thinks — and route each of those to somewhere it will be used.

## Why it matters

A project that ends without closure leaves three debts.

**The customer's debt.** Nobody said out loud what was delivered against what was agreed, so the account of the engagement is whatever each side remembers — and memories diverge in the direction of grievance.

**Our debt.** The lessons stay in the heads of the people who were there. The next project repeats the mistake, and the delivery model never improves, because the loop from a project's experience back into the model runs through this discipline. Without it, the model slowly becomes wrong.

**The commercial debt.** The next engagement is easiest to win from a delivered outcome nobody has written down. A closure report is the raw material for the reference case, and it is far cheaper to write now than to reconstruct in a year.

Closure is also where the value question is finally asked. The customer owns benefit realisation — but this is the moment we make it answerable.

## How it is done

**Write the closure report.** What was delivered against what was agreed. What changed along the way and why. Where time and budget landed against the estimate, with the variance explained rather than presented. What is left open, and who owns each open item.

**Check the outcome against the ladder.** Did the value arrive? Sometimes it is too early to know, and saying so is a legitimate answer — but then name when it *can* be known and who will look.

**Run the evaluation.** The Project Owner initiates it; the owner of the delivery model facilitates it. That second half matters: an evaluation facilitated by the project is a retrospective, and it stays in the project. Facilitated from outside, the lessons reach the model.

**Ask the customer, formally.** NPS or its equivalent — a number and a sentence. The sentence is the useful part.

**Route each finding.** A lesson with no destination is a lesson lost. Model change, discipline page, checklist line, or a note against the account — but somewhere.

**Do it while people are still there.** Closure written after the team has dispersed is an administrative exercise.

## What you can do at each level

| Level | What you can do |
|---|---|
| **Do it** | You can produce a closure report covering what was delivered against what was agreed, and collect the customer's feedback |
| **Show it** | You can write an honest variance — including the parts that reflect badly on us — and get a real answer from the customer rather than a polite one |
| **Build it** | You can turn closure into improvement: findings routed to named destinations and followed up until they land. You use the report to shape the next conversation with the account |
| **Own it** | You can run a closure on a difficult engagement so both sides leave with the same account of it. You feed the delivery model itself and change it when the evidence says so |

## How to get better

**Internally**

- The old delivery model's **Evaluation** material, and the detail that matters: the project initiates it, but the *owner of the delivery model* facilitates it. That is what gets the lessons out of the room.
- The **Business Value Ladder**, which is what the value question at closure is measured against.
- Read two closure reports from finished engagements — one that went well and one that did not. The second is worth more.

**Externally**

- Norman Kerth, *Project Retrospectives* — the origin of the prime directive, and still the best material on running a retrospective where people tell the truth.
- Esther Derby and Diana Larsen, *Agile Retrospectives* — practical formats, useful when the standard one has gone stale.
- The **Google SRE** postmortem culture material — blameless analysis, and specifically how to write up a failure so it is read rather than filed.

## Common failure

**Closure as a formality.** A template filled in for the file, describing a project that went broadly fine. Nobody reads it, and nothing changes.

**Only the successes.** A closure report with no variance and no lessons is not a record; it is marketing addressed to ourselves.

**Evaluation inside the project.** A retrospective run by the team improves the team. It does not improve the model, because nothing leaves the room.

**Deferring the value question.** *"It is too early to say"* is fine once, with a date attached. Said without a date, it means nobody will ever ask.

**Closing before the open items have owners.** Everything unfinished becomes support's problem by default, and support did not agree to it.

## Artefacts

Closure report · evaluation notes with findings routed · customer feedback and NPS · the closed **D100**, handed to whoever operates the solution · the list of open items with named owners.

## How it varies by approach

- **Scrum** and **Analysis**-driven builds have a natural end, and closure sits at it.
- **Use-case delivery** has none. This is where the discipline is most at risk and most needed — the quarterly business demo is the practical substitute, and something closure-shaped should still happen when a use-case programme winds down.
- **Kanban** and **Business Continuity** run continuously. Their equivalent is a periodic service review: same questions, on a cadence rather than at an end.

**Traditional vs agentic** makes little difference to the discipline, with one exception: when documentation is generated and kept current rather than written at the end, the closure report stops being an archaeology project and becomes a summary. That is a real gain, and it is worth using — the time saved should go into the evaluation, not into a shorter closure.
