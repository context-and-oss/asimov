# Allocation and staffing

*A **Scoping** discipline, maintained through Development.*

## Purpose

Get the people the engagement needs, when it needs them, at a mix of seniority that fits the work — and keep the booking honest so other engagements can plan around it.

## Why it matters

Allocation is where delivery meets the rest of the business. An engagement that books late gets whoever is free rather than whoever is right. One that books everyone early and releases nobody blocks work it will never do.

It is also where people are either developed or used. A project staffed entirely with seniors delivers well and teaches nobody. One staffed entirely with juniors teaches expensively. The mix is a decision, and leaving it to availability is how it gets made badly.

## How it is done

**Soft-book early, hard-book on commitment.** A soft booking says *we expect to need this person*; a hard booking says *we have committed to a customer*. Keeping the two distinct is what lets everyone else plan. Treating every soft booking as firm is how capacity disappears on paper while people sit idle.

**Take it to the staffing meeting.** There is a weekly rhythm for this, and bringing a need to it is faster than arranging it privately — and it lets the people who see the whole picture solve it.

**Mix seniority deliberately.** Decide what the engagement needs and what it can teach, rather than accepting whatever the calendar produces.

**Plan capacity and absence together.** Holiday, training and the other engagements someone is on are part of the allocation, not exceptions to it.

**Re-check when the plan changes.** A replan that does not touch the allocation has probably not been thought through.

## What you can do at each level

| Level | What you can do |
|---|---|
| **Do it** | You can state what the engagement needs — roles, effort, when — and keep your own bookings accurate |
| **Show it** | You can plan an engagement's staffing across its phases, distinguish soft from hard, and raise a gap early enough to be solved |
| **Build it** | You can staff for capability as well as capacity: a mix that delivers and develops, with cover for the single points of failure |
| **Own it** | You can resolve a staffing conflict between engagements so both outcomes are acceptable, and hold the line when a delivery is being staffed with whoever is left |

## How to get better

**Internally**

- The old delivery model's **Allocation** page, and the allocation checklist used at handover between phases.
- The **planning** discipline — the plan is what allocation is derived from, and a plan without an allocation behind it is a wish.
- Talk to a Team Lead about how staffing decisions actually get made. Most of this discipline is knowledge of the organisation.

**Externally**

- Tom DeMarco and Timothy Lister, *Peopleware* — on teams, and on why the productive unit is a team rather than a set of interchangeable people.
- Donald Reinertsen, *The Principles of Product Development Flow* — the queueing argument that high utilisation destroys throughput. The most useful counter to "everyone should be 100% booked".

## Common failure

**Hard-booking everything.** Capacity looks full, nothing can be planned, and the bookings turn out to be provisional anyway.

**Booking late.** The engagement gets availability rather than fit, and the estimate assumed fit.

**All-senior or all-junior.** Both are decisions; neither is usually the one that was intended.

**Ignoring the people question.** Allocation determines what someone spends six months learning. It is a development decision as much as a scheduling one.

## Artefacts

The allocation itself, in whatever system holds it · the allocation checklist at phase handover · capacity and absence planning. Feeds the **plan**, and follow-up on allocated against registered time belongs to the project's economy.

## How it varies by approach

Use-case delivery and Kanban assume a **standing team** — allocation is stable and the discipline is mostly about protecting it. Scrum needs capacity known per sprint. Prototyping needs a small number of the right people for a short time, which is the hardest thing to book. Launch and hyper care needs people named and genuinely available, which is the allocation most often assumed rather than made.

**Traditional vs agentic.** Fewer people deliver the same scope, which sounds like a simplification and is not. It concentrates risk: a two-person engagement has no cover, and the single points of failure that a larger team absorbed become visible. Allocation now has to plan for that explicitly.
