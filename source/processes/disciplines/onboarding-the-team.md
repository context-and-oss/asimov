# Onboarding the team

*A **Development** discipline, repeated whenever someone joins.*

## Purpose

Bring our own people up to speed on the engagement: the project's goal, the solution, how it will be run, and what each person is responsible for. The old model calls it the *internal kick-off*, which is the right description — it is the same session as the kick-off, held among ourselves.

## Why it matters

People join a project mid-flight far more often than at the start. Someone allocated in month four inherits a set of decisions, a customer relationship and a technical approach that everyone else absorbed gradually. Without deliberate onboarding they spend weeks reconstructing it, and the questions they do not know to ask stay unanswered.

There is a specific case the old model calls out and it is worth repeating: **when the people who will run the delivery were not in the scoping**, onboarding needs materially more time and continuing contact with whoever was. The understanding built during a scoping track is mostly not written down, and the handover of it is not a document.

## How it is done

**Decide the shape.** Sometimes one session; sometimes a series. Both are legitimate, and choosing deliberately beats defaulting to a single meeting because that is what fits a calendar.

**Cover what the person actually needs**: the project's goal and why the customer wants it, the solution in outline, how the project is run — meetings, tools, documents, how changes and issues are handled — and their own area of responsibility, stated rather than implied.

**Use the project handbook as the base**, adapted to this engagement. The handbook exists so nobody rebuilds the standard part each time.

**Give extra time where scoping context is missing.** If the solution-responsible or the project owner was not in the scoping track, arrange continuing contact with whoever was. One handover meeting will not carry it.

**Use the same approach for later joiners.** Someone arriving in month six needs more, not less, than someone who was there at kick-off.

## What you can do at each level

| Level | What you can do |
|---|---|
| **Do it** | You can onboard a new joiner using the handbook: goal, solution, ways of working, and what they own |
| **Show it** | You can judge what a particular person needs, and get them productive without them having to ask for the obvious. You notice what they were not told |
| **Build it** | You can set up an engagement so onboarding is cheap and repeatable — the material exists, is current, and does not depend on one person's memory |
| **Own it** | You can transfer the understanding from a scoping track to a delivery team that was not in it, which is the hardest version of this and the one most often skipped |

## How to get better

**Internally**

- The old delivery model's **Onboard team** page and the project handbook it points to.
- The **kick-off** discipline — the same content, a different audience.
- Ask the last person who joined an engagement what they wish they had been told. It is a short conversation and consistently useful.

**Externally**

- Michael Watkins, *The First 90 Days* — written about new roles, and the transition framework applies directly to joining a project.
- Tom DeMarco and Timothy Lister, *Peopleware* — on why teams take time to form and what disrupts them, which is the cost onboarding is trying to reduce.

## Common failure

**Access and nothing else.** Accounts granted, repository cloned, and no explanation of what any of it is for.

**Assuming the kick-off covered it.** The people who joined afterwards were not there.

**No owner.** Onboarding is everyone's job, so it is nobody's, and the new person works it out by interrupting people.

**Skipping the customer context.** Our people know what to build and nothing about who it is for, which is exactly the gap the FDE and ASWE pairing exists to close.

**Underestimating the scoping gap.** The most expensive version, because the missing understanding is invisible until a decision is made wrongly.

## Artefacts

The project handbook, adapted per engagement · onboarding notes for the individual · the responsibility checklist, which is where "what you own" is actually written down.

## How it varies by approach

Use-case delivery and Kanban run standing teams, so onboarding is occasional but deep — a joiner is joining a long-running relationship. Scrum onboards at sprint boundaries, which is the natural seam. Prototyping teams are small and short-lived, so onboarding is mostly context about the question being answered.

**Traditional vs agentic.** The technical onboarding gets shorter — the codebase can be explained by the tooling, and the conventions live in files rather than in people. The context onboarding gets *longer* and matters more: why this customer wants this, what was decided in scoping and why, what the constraints are. That is the part no tool supplies.
