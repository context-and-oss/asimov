# Change handling

*A **cross discipline**. Any commitment can change, in any phase.*

## Purpose

Take anything that alters what was agreed — scope, approach, timing, cost — and put it through the same route: logged, sized, presented, accepted, and folded into the total. The aim is not to resist change. It is to make sure change is a **decision** rather than a drift.

## Why it matters

Uncontrolled change is the most common way a delivery ends up over budget while everyone involved was working hard and acting in good faith. Each individual addition is small and reasonable. Nobody says no, because no single request is worth a fight. The total is never presented, so nobody sees it until the money runs out.

The discipline is also what protects the customer. A change accepted without a price is a change they did not get to weigh. Given the number, they will sometimes say no — and that is a service, not an obstacle.

There is a diagnostic in it too, and the old model states it plainly: **a project with many change requests indicates that the need was not well covered.** That is a signal about the scoping, and it should be taken back to the customer's project manager as a shared problem rather than absorbed as our administrative burden.

## How it is done

**Log every change**, including the small ones. Especially the small ones — the large ones announce themselves.

**Size it before discussing it.** A change without a number is an opinion. The estimate template carries the effort for handling changes, so the sizing is part of the work rather than an interruption to it.

**Present the effect on the total, not just on the item.** *"Two days"* invites a yes. *"Two days, taking us to 6 over the agreed 40, and pushing the test window into week 12"* invites a decision.

**Get acceptance in writing before building.** Verbal approval in a meeting is where change control quietly dies. The status meeting is the natural place to present and confirm changes, and that is the point of putting them there.

**Update the totals** — scope, budget and plan — once accepted. A log full of approved changes that never reach the baseline is a record of work nobody is tracking.

**Say no when no is the right answer.** Not every change should be accepted; some should be parked to a later phase, and some should be declined because they do not serve any rung of the value ladder.

**Watch the volume.** Rising change requests early is information: the requirement was not understood. That conversation belongs with the customer's project manager while there is still time to act on it.

## What you can do at each level

| Level | What you can do |
|---|---|
| **Do it** | You can recognise that something is a change, log it, size it, and get it accepted before building it |
| **Show it** | You can present a change so the customer can decide — effect on total scope, budget and plan, not just the item. You hold the line when the request arrives informally |
| **Build it** | You can set up change handling so it is fast enough that people use it rather than route around it, and connect it to the plan and the estimate so the baseline stays true |
| **Own it** | You can have the conversation about why change requests are accumulating, with the customer, without it becoming a blame exercise. You can decline a change and keep the relationship |

## How to get better

**Internally**

- The old delivery model's **Managing changes** page, including the diagnostic about what a high volume of change requests means.
- The estimate template's provision for handling changes, and the practice of confirming changes at the status meeting.
- The **Hyper care** discipline's triage — defect, change or training. It is the same judgement under time pressure, and the hardest place to make it.

**Externally**

- Karl Wiegers, *Software Requirements* — the chapters on change control. Practical, and good on the difference between a change process and a change bureaucracy.
- Steve McConnell, *Software Estimation* — on re-estimation and on why the original number keeps being quoted long after it stopped applying.
- **ITIL change management** — for the vocabulary of standard, normal and emergency changes. Heavier than we need, but the classification idea is useful when volume is high.

## Common failure

**Scope creep by accumulation.** Twenty small yeses, none of them logged, discovered as a budget problem.

**Building first, agreeing later.** The work is done, the invoice is the first the customer hears of it, and the conversation is now about money rather than about value.

**A process so heavy it gets bypassed.** If logging a change costs more than making it, people will make it. The process has to be proportionate or it will be a fiction.

**Presenting the item, not the total.** The customer says yes twelve times without ever seeing the twelve.

**Treating a high volume as an administrative problem.** It is a scoping signal, and answering it with better paperwork misses it.

**Never saying no.** A change process that has approved everything is a logging system, not a control.

## Artefacts

The change log, held where the engagement lives and reviewed weekly. Accepted changes update the **estimate**, the **plan** and the agreed scope — and appear in the **status report** as open, approved and closed. Where a change alters what will be built, it also updates the **specification**.

## Where it is exercised

| Phase | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Proposal** | Changes to the offer during negotiation, approved on our side before being sent |
| **Scoping** | Scope is still forming, so most movement is not a change — but the moment something is agreed, it becomes one |
| **Development** | Its home. The log runs weekly |
| **Operations** | Becomes the request process, and the defect-or-change judgement dominates |

**By approach:** Analysis and fixed price need this most — the whole commercial position rests on the baseline holding. Scrum handles change between sprints and should not handle it within one. Use-case delivery absorbs change by design, so the discipline shifts from controlling scope to keeping the roadmap honest. Kanban has no scope to protect; its equivalent is the replenishment decision.

**Traditional vs agentic.** A change is cheaper to *build*, which makes it more tempting to accept without pricing — and that is the trap. Cheap to build is not the same as free: it still displaces something, still needs testing and documentation, and still moves the total. If anything, the discipline needs holding more firmly, because its usual justification — "that would take a week" — has lost its force.
