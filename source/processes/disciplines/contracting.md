# Contracting

*A **Proposal** discipline, revisited whenever the agreement changes.*

## Purpose

Establish what we have agreed and on what terms. Two documents do the work: a **framework agreement** setting the terms of cooperation, and a **project agreement** describing the specific services. One is largely standard; the other is written for this engagement.

## Why it matters

The contract is what everyone falls back on when the relationship stops being enough. It is read carefully exactly once — when something has gone wrong — and by then it either says what both sides thought it said or it does not.

Getting it right is also a delivery concern, not only a commercial one. The delivery team must be able to deliver on the terms, and a term nobody on the delivery side ever read is a term nobody is meeting. The responsibility checklist makes this explicit: understanding the contract and being able to deliver on its terms is a named line.

## How it is done

**The framework agreement** sets the general rights and ground rules for the cooperation. Most of its annexes are standard — data processing, prices, support terms. Where the customer wants different terms, they go in the "other terms" section rather than being edited into the body. If the customer insists on their own template, it goes to review and needs approval at director level.

**The project agreement** describes the specific services: a short description of the solution or the tasks, expected start and end dates, prerequisites, and the collaboration model. Where a framework agreement exists, the project agreement sits inside it. Its sub-annexes are the solution and project descriptions — which means the delivery documents are contractually attached, and should be read as such.

**Three steps, and they are separate on purpose.** Preparation of the agreement · negotiation, including any change to the material, approved at director level · signature, by an authorised signatory only. Signing is digital, and the agreement is stored where contracts are kept, not in the project folder.

**Read it on the delivery side before it is signed.** The technical terms in particular: response times, environments, dependencies on the customer, anything that assumes an access or an approval we have not confirmed.

## What you can do at each level

| Level | What you can do |
|---|---|
| **Do it** | You can read an agreement and say whether the delivery can meet its terms, and flag the ones that concern you |
| **Show it** | You can prepare a project agreement from a scoping output — scope, dates, prerequisites, collaboration model — so it says what was actually agreed |
| **Build it** | You can shape the agreement so the contract type and the delivery approach fit each other, and get the prerequisites written down rather than assumed |
| **Own it** | You can negotiate terms with a customer, know which ones are worth conceding and which are not, and keep the relationship through a hard negotiation |

## How to get better

**Internally**

- The old delivery model's agreement pages — framework agreement, project agreement, signing.
- The **selection guide**'s contract-fit table: which approach works under which contract, and where the friction is.
- Sit in on a contract negotiation before you run one.

**Externally**

- Peter Block, *Flawless Consulting* — the contracting chapters, which are about the psychological contract as much as the legal one. The most useful thing on this list.
- Roger Fisher and William Ury, *Getting to Yes* — principled negotiation, and specifically separating the people from the problem.

## Common failure

**Nobody on the delivery side reads it.** The terms are met by luck until they are not.

**Prerequisites left implicit.** Access, environments, customer availability — assumed in the plan, absent from the agreement, and unenforceable when they do not arrive.

**Signature by the wrong person.** Enthusiasm is not authority, and an agreement signed by someone without it is a problem discovered late.

**The delivery documents attached without being read as contract.** The solution and project descriptions are annexes. What they say is what we owe.

## Artefacts

Framework agreement with its annexes · project agreement with the solution and project descriptions attached · the signed copy in the contract store. Changes to the agreement run through **change handling** and must be documented in writing.

## How it varies by approach

The contract type constrains which approaches are viable — see the selection guide. Fixed price needs a locked scope, so it pairs with Analysis. Subscription pairs with Discovery and use-case delivery. Kanban's Service Level Expectation has no contract shape yet.

**Traditional vs agentic.** One thing is genuinely new and is easy to miss: **the zone agreement.** What AI may access is a contractual matter, not only a technical one, and it belongs in the agreement rather than in a conversation. The Zone 2 one-pager exists for this.
