# Phase 2. Scoping

**Purpose:** turn needs into requirements and a plan the customer can decide on. The output is a *basis for decision*, not a solution.

The approaches differ in **what they lock**:

| Approach | Locks | Output | Estimation unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Scoping & Planning** | Scope known, plan set, still adjustable | **A100** + detailed project plan + estimate | Hours, at feature → PBI/task level |
| **Discovery (workshops)** | Nothing — locks the *direction* and the MVP use case | MVP use case definition, backlog, feature roadmap | **Effort points** on PBI |
| **Analysis** | Scope, completely | Requirements specification + solution design | Hours, closest to waterfall |
| **Onboarding/handover** | Takeover, not new build | Takeover documentation, access, **E100** IT environment | — |

*Onboarding/handover here means **taking over an existing solution** somebody else built — not to be confused with "handover to operations" in Phase 4, which is us handing ours off. Different direction, different approach.*

**The estimation unit is not cosmetic.** Hours bind to time-and-materials and fixed price; effort points bind to subscription. When approaches mix freely, the scoping approach and the contract type must still be able to talk to each other.

**Starting with prototyping instead.** A project may skip these approaches and start straight into prototyping. The model permits it and warns about it:

- **Recommendation:** run one of the approaches above first to establish an overall picture. Prototyping answers a question well; it does not produce an overview.
- **When starting with prototyping anyway makes sense:** a new idea, market validation, disagreement among stakeholders about what should be built, or requirements nobody can write down before they have seen something.
- **What to watch:** a prototype looks like a finished product, and customers routinely judge it closer to done than it is.
- **How to align expectations:** verbally *and* in writing, in the contract or in other documentation both parties have approved. It must state plainly that the prototype is not a deliverable, what remains unclarified, and what has to happen before a finished solution can be priced.

The prototyping approach itself is described under [Development](development.md).

**Cross-cutting method, valid for all approaches.** The workshop guide (before/during/after — define purpose and expected output first, participants must hold a mandate to decide, the pizza rule, check-out with two concrete to-dos and named owners) and the estimation philosophy (break down before estimating; whole numbers or ranges, never "807.5 hours"; include clarification, documentation, training and handover, not just build).

**Gate out of Scoping.** Approved basis for decision · contract type agreed · **Development approaches chosen** · zone and level agreed · staffing · customer meeting cadence agreed.

**Traditional vs agentic:** the activities are identical. A100 and D100 used to be written by hand; agentically they are generated wholly or partly from workshop notes, transcripts and existing code, then edited and approved by a person.
