# The discipline catalogue

A **discipline** is a practice you must have under control on an engagement, and one you can **get better at**. Both halves matter. It is not only "be rigorous about this"; it is a skill with a beginner and a master, the way sprinting is a discipline in athletics.

That second half is what makes this catalogue worth building. Disciplines are the things a competency map can actually measure: more concrete than a role, more durable than a tool.

**Disciplines are independent of approach.** Risk, estimation, expectation-matching and change handling are needed whether the work runs as Scrum, Kanban, prototyping or use-case delivery. The approach decides *when and how often* a discipline is exercised; it does not decide *whether*.

## Cross disciplines

Some disciplines are anchored to a phase: deployment and hyper care belong to Development, closure reporting to Operations, change readiness to Proposal. Others are exercised in **every** phase, and describing them inside a phase would mean describing them four times.

Those are **cross disciplines**, and their pages carry a *Where it is exercised* section instead of *How it varies by approach*:

| Cross discipline | Why it is cross |
|---|---|
| **Workshop facilitation** | Every phase produces understanding or decisions with other people in a room |
| **Expectation matching** | Runs continuously; has no artefact of its own and no phase of its own |
| **Status reporting** | From the first week to the closure report |
| **Risk management** | A risk register opened in Proposal and closed at handover |
| **Issue handling** | Wherever a question needs an answer and a trace |
| **Change handling** | Any commitment can change, in any phase |
| **Estimation** | Scoping estimates a delivery; Development re-estimates a feature; Operations sizes a request |
| **Planning** | The plan exists at three levels and across all four phases |
| **Cadence and meeting structure** | Set once, exercised throughout |
| **Value laddering** | Built in Scoping, referenced in Development, the scorecard in Operations |

The rest are phase disciplines. The distinction matters for the site: cross disciplines get linked *from* the phase pages rather than described *in* them.

## How a discipline page is built

Every page has the same shape, so the disciplines stay comparable: what it is for, why it matters, how it is done, what you can do at each level, how to get better, how it commonly goes wrong, what it produces, and how it varies by approach.

**The proficiency ladder uses the company Growth Framework's own levels: Do it · Show it · Build it · Own it.** Inventing a second scale next to the one the company already uses would be a gift to nobody, and it means the competency map can read these pages directly.

## Three kinds of thing, kept apart

The old delivery model drew all of these as identical boxes, which is why they were easy to confuse.

| Kind | What it is | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|
| **Discipline** | A recurring practice, and a skill | This catalogue |
| **Artefact** | Something produced: a document, a plan, an agreement | The document chain |
| **Setup step** | A one-off, tool-bound action | An engagement checklist |

**An artefact usually has a discipline behind it.** Writing a contract or laying out a project plan is a skill, not a form-filling exercise. So the two are paired, not merged:

- the **artefact** definition answers *what must be clarified in it*
- the **discipline** answers *how you produce and maintain it well*

For example: artefact `Project plan` ↔ discipline **Planning**; artefact `Project agreement` ↔ discipline **Contracting**; artefacts `A100 / D100 / D101` ↔ discipline **Specification**.

Setup steps (creating the project in the finance system, the DevOps project, the Teams site, booking allocation) are deliberately *not* disciplines. They change whenever the tooling changes, and putting them in the model would date it.

## The catalogue

Twenty-eight disciplines, grouped by where in an engagement they are first needed. Ten are **cross** disciplines, exercised in every phase; the rest are anchored to one.

### Understanding and framing

| Discipline | What it covers |
|---|---|
| **Stakeholder analysis** | Who decides, who blocks, who must be brought along |
| **Outcome definition** | Turning aspiration into a measurable business outcome |
| **Change readiness** | Whether the customer's organisation can absorb what we build |
| **Value laddering** | The three rungs: Purpose (why) · Goals (what) · Implementation (how). Plus success criteria, and the four outcome categories: revenue, costs, compliance, stakeholder satisfaction |
| **Customer analysis** | Reading the customer: stakeholders, maturity, finances, appetite for change |
| **Workshop facilitation** *(cross)* | Before, during and after a workshop, where most of the quality is decided before anyone arrives |
| **Expectation matching** | Keeping the customer's picture and ours the same |

### Planning and committing

| Discipline | What it covers |
|---|---|
| **Estimation** | Break down before estimating · whole numbers or ranges, never "807.5 hours" · include clarification, documentation, training, handover · feature → PBI/task, mixable |
| **Planning** | The project plan at three levels (monthly high-level, week/sprint, daily around go-live), plus sprint themes and an archive for versions · artefact: project plan |
| **Contracting** | Framework agreement (terms, annexes) and project agreement (the specific services), changes, signing · artefacts: the agreements |
| **Allocation and staffing** | Soft-book early, hard-book on commitment, mix seniority |
| **Specification** | What must be built, how, and how we know it works · artefacts: A100 / D100 / D101 |

### Running the work

| Discipline | What it covers |
|---|---|
| **Cadence and meeting structure** | Which meetings exist, who attends, what each produces, with Scrum meetings and management meetings side by side |
| **Steering** | Weekly progress meeting, monthly steering committee, monthly plan review |
| **Onboarding the team** | Getting a consultant productive on this engagement, the "internal kick-off" |
| **Onboarding the customer** | Teaching the customer our meeting structure, document model, change handling and tools |
| **Kick-off** | Starting the project with a common understanding: goal, success criteria, plan, first risk list |
| **Change handling** | Every change logged, priced and accepted before it is built |
| **Risk management** | Any uncertain event that could hit scope, schedule, resources, cost or quality · likelihood × impact scored · mitigation planned · **and how to have the difficult conversation about it** · artefact: P500 |
| **Issue handling** | The clarification log: a paper trail that never replaces a conversation. Rule of thumb: if a direct question goes unanswered, log it |
| **Status reporting** | Weekly update on time, budget and scope. *"It is MUCH better to communicate more than less"*. A project can succeed over budget if contact stayed close and honest. **Adapt the form to the task; never drop the paper trail** · artefacts: P300 full report, P400 status email |
| **Testing** | Test levels, acceptance, who signs off |

### Shipping and finishing

| Discipline | What it covers |
|---|---|
| **Deployment** | Cut-over, the deployment procedure, the rollback path · artefacts: deployment plan, E200 |
| **Hyper care** | The intensive period after go-live: staffing, response, exit criteria |
| **Adoption** | Training, superusers, communication, benefit realisation |
| **Handover to operations** | What the receiving side must get, and when it counts as accepted · artefacts: BC100, E100 |
| **Closure reporting** *(the old model called it Evaluation)* | Ending an engagement deliberately: what was delivered against what was agreed, whether the value arrived, and the retrospective that feeds the model rather than only the project · artefact: closure report |

### Confirm Scope

| Discipline | What it covers |
|---|---|
| **Confirm Scope** | The closing workshop of a scoping track. We present back our understanding of the solution and of the project it takes to get there, so the customer can see that we understood them, what it will take, and decide the next step |
