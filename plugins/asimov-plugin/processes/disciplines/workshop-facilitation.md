# Workshop facilitation

*A **cross discipline**. It is exercised in every phase and under every approach, so it is described once here rather than repeated wherever a session happens.*

## Purpose

Design and run a session that produces what it was called for. Facilitation is not chairing a meeting — it is deciding in advance what the session must produce, choosing who has to be in the room for that to be possible, and then holding the session to it while everyone else follows the conversation where it wants to go.

The discipline has three parts and they are unequal: **before, during, after**. Most of the quality is decided before anyone arrives.

## Why it matters

Workshops are how a consultancy learns things. Almost everything we know about a customer's business arrives through a session someone ran, and the quality of that session sets the ceiling on everything downstream — the scope, the estimate, the specification, the solution.

A badly run workshop is not neutral. It costs the customer's most expensive people half a day, produces a set of notes nobody trusts, and teaches the room that our sessions are not worth clearing the calendar for. The second workshop then gets delegates instead of deciders, and the scoping track never recovers.

There is also a simple economic point. A workshop with eight senior people in it is one of the most expensive hours we ever bill, on both sides of the table. It deserves preparation proportional to that.

## How it is done

### Before — where the quality is decided

**Define the purpose first, and the expected output with it.** Not the topic — the output. *"That we, on the basis of the submitted material, discuss, adapt and decide the future process for X, along with the associated data points."* Everything else in the preparation follows from this sentence, and if it cannot be written, the workshop is not ready to be booked.

**Choose the meeting type deliberately.** Decision · information · problem-solving · knowledge-sharing. They need different preparation, different rooms and different people, and a session trying to be two of them usually achieves neither.

**Pick the audience from the purpose, not from the org chart.** Decision-makers want specific problems, obstacles and finances. Subject-matter experts want process, data and clarification. The project group wants progress, burn rate and status. Aim the content at whoever is actually coming.

**Limit the number of participants.** The old guide's test is the good one: *the pizza rule* — if two pizzas cannot feed the room, there are too many people in it. Too many participants makes decisions hard and the session unmanageable.

**Check that someone in the room holds a mandate.** If decisions have to be made and nobody present can make them, postpone the workshop. Running it anyway produces a recommendation nobody asked for.

**Set the time and the time frame.** Enough, and not too much. Put heavy decisions in the morning.

**Book preparation time in your own calendar** — and a second slot in case preparation takes longer than expected. This is the step people skip, and it is the one that determines everything above.

**Plan the involvement.** Small, precise questions with short answers, written down so participants can read the instruction. *"What value should the project create?" · "What does this project mean for your working day?" · "What is your main concern about it?"* Take a round of the table before deciding anything, so everyone has spoken once.

**Build the agenda as a shape:** welcome · check-in · introduction · purpose · review of the workshop · content · round-off and summary · check-out · next step. Visualise it as a timeline.

**Plan the timetable in 30–45 minute blocks with breaks.** Prepare backup content. Send an invitation that says what participants must prepare. Test the technology in advance — this matters most for virtual sessions, which is where the old guide puts its emphasis throughout.

### During

**Immediately before:** get into the right headspace, reconcile the agenda and the internal roles, check that preparation is done, test the technology again.

**Agree the internal roles.** A facilitator, someone on the technology, someone taking notes. One person doing all three does none of them well.

**Start on time.** Check in — make room for small talk and a short round of introductions. Then state the rules, the purpose and the agenda before any content.

**Record the session, with consent**, when that helps the notes.

**Hold the focus.** Involve the room roughly every five minutes; present in blocks of no more than ten. When a good idea arrives that is not on the agenda, park it visibly rather than following it or dismissing it.

**Take notes as you go**, and let the room see them being taken.

**Check out properly.** Summarise what was decided, agree **two concrete to-dos with named owners**, ask the customer for feedback on the session itself, and state the next step. A workshop that ends with "we'll send something round" has ended badly.

### After

**Adjust the notes while it is fresh** — expand the abbreviations, group them logically. Notes written for yourself are not notes.

**Debrief internally** before the impressions fade.

**Send the notes and the to-dos to the participants**, the same day where possible. The old guide's phrase for this is worth keeping: *be a boutique consultant.*

**Debrief with the customer's project manager** separately. What they say afterwards is often different from what they said in the room.

**Prepare the next step**, so the momentum the session created is not spent waiting.

## What you can do at each level

| Level | What you can do |
|---|---|
| **Do it** | You can prepare and run a workshop from a defined purpose: agenda, invitation, timetable, notes taken and sent afterwards with to-dos and owners. You keep to time |
| **Show it** | You can write the purpose and expected output yourself, choose the meeting type and the right participants for it, and design involvement that gets the quiet people talking. You handle the session going off-plan without losing the output |
| **Build it** | You can design a *track* of workshops that builds towards a decision, not a series of separate sessions. You get a mandate into the room before the session rather than discovering its absence in it, and you can run a room where the participants disagree with each other |
| **Own it** | You can facilitate a session where the decision is genuinely contested and the stakes are high, and get a real decision out of it. You set the standard others facilitate against and coach them through their first difficult room |

## How to get better

**Internally**

- The **workshop guide** in the old delivery model — 36 slides, structured as before / during / after. It is the single strongest piece of material we have, and this page is a condensation of it rather than a replacement.
- The **teaching deck** *How we (should) deliver*, for worked exercises and a real-world example of presenting understanding back to a customer.
- **Co-facilitate before you facilitate.** Take the note-taker role in someone else's session first. This discipline is learned by watching, and the note-taker seat is the best seat for watching from.

**Externally**

- Sam Kaner, *Facilitator's Guide to Participatory Decision-Making* — the standard work, and specifically good on the messy middle of a session where a group has stopped agreeing and has not yet decided.
- Priya Parker, *The Art of Gathering* — on purpose-first design, and the strongest argument anywhere for deciding what a gathering is *for* before deciding anything else.
- Dave Gray, Sunni Brown and James Macanufo, *Gamestorming* — a practical library of exercises when the standard round-the-table is not producing anything.

## Common failure

**Booking the room before writing the purpose.** Everything downstream then gets decided by the calendar rather than by what the session needs to produce.

**No mandate in the room.** The most expensive failure, because it is only discovered at the moment the decision was supposed to happen.

**Skipping preparation because the topic is familiar.** Familiarity with the subject is not preparation for the session.

**One person facilitating, presenting, and taking notes.** The notes suffer first, then the facilitation.

**Following the interesting tangent.** It feels like value in the room and reads as a lost afternoon in the notes.

**Notes that never arrive.** The single most common failure, and it undoes the whole session: what is not written down and sent is not what the customer remembers.

## Artefacts

The invitation and pre-read · the agenda · the notes, adjusted and circulated · the to-do list with named owners · the parked-items list. Workshop output feeds whatever the session was for — **A100**, the MVP use case definition, the requirements specification, the risk list.

## Where it is exercised

Being a cross discipline, it is not anchored to a phase. It appears wherever a decision or an understanding has to be produced with other people in the room.

| Phase | Where |
|---|---|
| **Proposal** | Change readiness conversations; the clarification sessions before an offer |
| **Scoping** | The workshop tracks in Scoping & Planning · Discovery workshops · requirement sessions in Analysis · **Confirm Scope**, which is the most demanding session of the phase |
| **Development** | Kick-off · refinement where the customer is present · demos · prototype review sessions · retrospectives |
| **Operations** | Handover sessions · service reviews · evaluation |

**It varies most by meeting type, not by approach.** A Discovery workshop is a problem-solving session; Confirm Scope is a decision session; a demo is closer to information with a decision attached. Naming which one it is beforehand does most of the work.

**Traditional vs agentic.** The room is human either way. Two things change in the preparation and the follow-up: material can be drafted from previous sessions, transcripts and existing documents rather than built from scratch, and notes can be turned into a structured record — or into the first draft of a specification — in minutes rather than the following morning. Both free time that should go into the part that cannot be automated: deciding what the session must produce, and getting the right people to turn up.
