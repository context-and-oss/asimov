# Change readiness

## Purpose

Assess whether the customer can actually take on the change: whether the people affected have the capacity, the mandate and the appetite, and whether the organisation has survived changes like this before. It is a judgement about *them*, made before we commit to a delivery, and it belongs in Proposal because it changes what we should offer.

## Why it matters

**The faster we build, the more often the customer's capacity is the constraint rather than ours.** That sentence is the whole argument. When delivery took nine months, the organisation had nine months to get ready whether it planned to or not. When the same scope lands in ten weeks, readiness stops being a background process and becomes the thing that decides whether any value is realised.

The cost of skipping it is specific and familiar: a solution delivered on time, on budget and to specification, sitting unused because the department it was for was mid-reorganisation, or because the two people who understood the old process left, or because nobody had the authority to change how the work was actually done.

It also protects the commercial arrangement, and it protects both sides of it. An outcome-based price set against an organisation that cannot absorb the outcome is a bet neither party wins: the supplier carries a cost it did not price for, and the customer still does not get the outcome it paid for.

## How it is done

**Assess five things.** None needs a survey; all need an honest conversation and someone willing to write down an uncomfortable answer.

| Dimension | The question |
|---|---|
| **Sponsorship** | Is there a sponsor with the authority to change how work is done, and the attention to spend on it? |
| **Capacity** | Do the people affected have room? What else is landing on them this quarter? |
| **Track record** | What happened the last two times this organisation changed something? Ask for the failure, not the success |
| **Clarity of the "why"** | Can the people affected say why this is happening, in their own words? If only the sponsor can, it has not landed |
| **Ability to decide** | How fast can this organisation make a decision it has not made before? This is the number that constrains every approach |

**Score it plainly and say so.** Red, amber, green per dimension, with a sentence each. A grade with no sentence is a number nobody trusts.

**Act on it in the offer.** A low score is not a reason to walk away — it is a reason to change the shape. Smaller first slice, a pilot before the rollout, adoption work priced in rather than assumed, a slower cadence chosen deliberately. The failure mode is scoring it and then proposing exactly what we would have proposed anyway.

**Say it out loud to the customer.** This is the discipline's hardest part and its main value. A customer told early that their organisation looks stretched will sometimes fix it. A customer told at go-live will remember that we knew.

## What you can do at each level

| Level | What you can do |
|---|---|
| **Do it** | You can run the five dimensions with the customer and record an honest score with a sentence behind each |
| **Show it** | You can get past the polite answer — ask about the last change that failed and listen to the reply without rescuing them from it |
| **Build it** | You can turn a weak score into a different proposal: a different first slice, a different cadence, adoption work priced in. You can explain to the customer why this shape and not the one they asked for |
| **Own it** | You can tell a sponsor their organisation is not ready, keep the relationship, and be thanked for it later. You can coach someone else through that conversation before they have to have it |

## How to get better

**Internally**

- The **Adoption** material in the old delivery model — training, superusers, communication planning and benefit realisation. Readiness and adoption are the same problem seen from two ends.
- The **Stakeholder analysis** discipline, which supplies half the input to this one.
- Ask around about an engagement that delivered and was not used. Every consultancy has them; ours are the most instructive reading available.

**Externally**

- John Kotter, *Leading Change* — the eight-step model. Dated in places, still the common vocabulary, and worth knowing because customers will use it.
- The **Prosci ADKAR model** — awareness, desire, knowledge, ability, reinforcement. A practical checklist for where a change will stall.
- Chip and Dan Heath, *Switch* — on why organisations that want to change still don't, which is the question this discipline is really asking.

## Common failure

**Doing it and ignoring it.** The assessment exists, the score is amber, and the proposal is unchanged. This is the most common failure by a distance.

**Confusing enthusiasm with readiness.** The sponsor's excitement says nothing about the capacity of the twelve people who must change their Tuesday.

**Asking only the sponsor.** A sponsor sees the intent of the change. The people who will absorb it are the ones who know what else is landing on them that quarter, so they are the ones to ask.

**Treating it as a gate rather than a design input.** It is not a pass/fail on the customer. It is information about what shape of engagement will work.

## Artefacts

A change-readiness assessment, produced in Proposal and referenced in Scoping when the approach is chosen. It informs the **Adoption** plan and, together with **Stakeholder analysis**, shapes the phasing of the delivery. It should be revisited if the customer's organisation moves.

## How it varies by approach

It determines the approach more than the approach determines it.

- **Low readiness** points to prototyping first, or a bounded pilot, or use-case delivery where each slice is small enough to absorb.
- **High readiness with a locked scope** is where Analysis and a fixed price work well.
- **Any approach with a fast cadence** — Kanban's replenishment, use-case delivery's continuous flow — assumes an organisation that can decide quickly. That assumption is exactly what this discipline tests.

There is no meaningful traditional-versus-agentic distinction in *how* it is done. There is a large one in *how much it matters*, and that is the reason it is on this list at all.
