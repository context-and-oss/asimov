# Adoption

*Spans **Development** and **Operations**. Planned during the build, exercised around go-live, continued afterwards.*

## Purpose

Get the people the solution was built for to use it, well, and keep using it. Adoption covers stakeholder work, training in its various forms, communication, superuser communities, help during testing, benefit realisation, and the continuing work of adopting new capabilities as the platform evolves.

## Why it matters

A solution nobody uses has produced no value, and the delivery was still paid for. That is the whole argument, and it is why adoption is not an optional add-on to a delivery — it is the last stretch of it.

It matters more now than it did. When delivery took nine months, the organisation had nine months of exposure to the change. When the same scope lands in ten weeks, the people affected have had ten weeks — and the constraint on realising value moves from our build speed to their absorption speed.

It is also what makes an outcome-based price safe. If the price was set against a business outcome and nobody uses the thing, the outcome does not arrive regardless of how well it was built.

## How it is done

**Plan it during the build, not after.** Adoption arranged in the last two weeks is a training session and a launch email.

**Start from the stakeholders.** Who has to change what they do, and what will make that hard. This is the end-user analysis that customer analysis explicitly excludes.

**Choose the training forms deliberately.** Written material, video, sessions — different audiences need different things, and video that nobody watches is a cost with no benefit.

**Plan the communication.** What is changing, when, why, and what it means for the person reading. Sent by someone in their organisation rather than by us where possible.

**Build a superuser community.** The most effective adoption mechanism available, and the cheapest: people who ask a colleague get an answer, and people who have to raise a ticket often just carry on as before.

**Help during testing.** Customer testing is also the first real training, and treating it that way gets two things from one activity.

**Follow benefit realisation.** The customer owns it; we make it visible. This is where the value ladder's bottom rung gets checked.

**Keep going.** Platforms change, capabilities arrive, new people join. Continuous adoption is a service, and it is one of the more defensible things to sell.

## What you can do at each level

| Level | What you can do |
|---|---|
| **Do it** | You can deliver planned adoption activities — training material, sessions, support during testing |
| **Show it** | You can plan adoption for a solution: who needs what, in which form, and when. You spot where resistance will come from before it arrives |
| **Build it** | You can design adoption into the delivery so it runs alongside the build rather than after it, and build a superuser community that outlasts our involvement |
| **Own it** | You can turn around an adoption that has failed — a live solution nobody uses — which is a different and harder job than doing it right first time |

## How to get better

**Internally**

- The old delivery model's **Adoption** page and its catalogue: stakeholder analysis, training, communication, superusers, test assistance, benefit realisation, continuous adoption.
- The **change readiness** discipline, which predicts how hard this will be, and **hyper care**, where most of what arrives is an adoption problem in disguise.
- The **value laddering** discipline — adoption is what turns the ladder's middle rungs into its top one.

**Externally**

- Everett Rogers, *Diffusion of Innovations* — the canonical work, and the origin of the early-adopter idea that most adoption plans use without knowing where it came from.
- The **Prosci ADKAR** model — awareness, desire, knowledge, ability, reinforcement. A practical diagnostic for which of the five is missing when adoption stalls.
- Chip and Dan Heath, *Switch* — on why people who want to change still don't, and what to do about it.

## Common failure

**Adoption as a training session.** One session, one recording, and a launch email.

**Planned after go-live.** By then the first impression has been formed, and first impressions of internal systems are durable.

**Training the enthusiastic.** The people who turn up are the people who did not need it.

**No superusers.** Every question becomes a ticket, most questions never get asked, and people revert to the old way quietly.

**Treating low usage as a user problem.** It is usually a design, communication or capacity problem, and the fix is not another reminder email.

**Stopping at go-live.** Adoption of the *next* capability needs the same work as adoption of the first.

## Artefacts

The adoption plan · training material in its various forms · the communication plan · the superuser arrangement · benefit-realisation measurements where we are helping with them.

## How it varies by approach

Scrum and Analysis-driven builds concentrate adoption around a single go-live, which makes it a distinct, plannable stretch — and easy to under-resource. Use-case delivery adopts continuously, one use case at a time, which is gentler on the organisation and easy to forget to plan at all. Kanban and Business Continuity carry adoption of changes as part of the service.

**Traditional vs agentic.** Material can be generated and kept current far more cheaply, which removes the usual excuse for out-of-date training documentation. What does not change is the human part: getting people to work differently is the same job it always was, and it is now the larger share of the elapsed time between starting and realising value.
