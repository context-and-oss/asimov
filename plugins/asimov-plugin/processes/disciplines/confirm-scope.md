# Confirm Scope

## Purpose

The workshop that closes a scoping track. We present back what we have understood: the solution to be built, and the project it will take to get there. It is a presentation, not a discovery session — the discovery already happened, and this is where it is confirmed or corrected.

Three things have to come out of it:

1. **That we have understood them** — their business, their challenge, and what they actually need rather than what was first asked for.
2. **What it will take** — the shape of the solution and the shape of the project: phases, approach, effort, what we need from them.
3. **A conversation about the next step** — which is a decision, not a formality.

## Why it matters

It is the gate between understanding and committing, and it is the last cheap moment to be wrong. A misunderstanding caught here costs a conversation. The same misunderstanding caught in Development costs a change request, and caught at go-live it costs the relationship.

It is also where trust is either established or not. A customer who watches their own problem described back to them accurately — including the parts they found hard to say — will believe the rest of the proposal. One who hears a generic solution pitched at them will spend the whole engagement checking our work.

And it protects the estimate. The price that follows a scoping track is only defensible if both sides agree on what was scoped. Without this session, the estimate rests on our reading alone.

## How it is done

**Prepare it as a presentation, not a discussion.** The material is written in advance and the session is run from it. That is what makes it a confirmation. A session that opens with *"so, what do you think we should build?"* has skipped the work.

**Cover three things, in this order.**

| Part | Content |
|---|---|
| **Our understanding** | The business challenge in their words, the stakeholders, the constraints we found, and what surprised us. Say what we heard, including the uncomfortable parts |
| **The solution and the project** | What will be built, at the level of features and capabilities rather than screens. How it will be delivered: which approach, which phases, roughly what effort, and what we need from them |
| **The next step** | What we propose happens now, what it costs, and what a decision requires from them |

**Say what is *not* in scope**, plainly and in writing. The exclusions are the part people remember incorrectly, and this is the last session where naming them is cheap.

**Name what is still open.** A scoping track never closes every question. The open list, with owners and a route to closing each, is more valuable than pretending it is complete.

**Bring the right people.** The decision-makers, not their delegates — this session exists to produce a decision, and a room that cannot decide turns it into another workshop.

**Record what was confirmed and send it the same day.** Verbal agreement in a workshop is not agreement in month three.

## What you can do at each level

| Level | What you can do |
|---|---|
| **Do it** | You can run the session from material someone else prepared: present the understanding, the solution and the project, and capture what the customer confirms or corrects |
| **Show it** | You can build the material yourself from a scoping track's output, and present it so the customer recognises their own problem in it. You state exclusions and open questions without being pushed, and you leave with a written confirmation |
| **Build it** | You can design the session for the room — knowing which parts to emphasise for which stakeholders, where the disagreement will be, and how to surface it deliberately rather than discovering it later. You handle a correction mid-session and adjust without losing the thread |
| **Own it** | You can run this with an executive audience where the decision is genuinely in doubt, hold a conversation about a scope the customer does not want to hear, and still get to a decision. You set the standard for what a confirmation session must contain, and coach others through their first ones |

## How to get better

**Internally**

- The **workshop guide** in the old delivery model — 36 slides on preparing, running and following up a workshop. It is the strongest material we have, and everything in it applies here: define the purpose and expected output first, make sure participants hold a mandate to decide, involve the room regularly, and close with agreed to-dos and owners.
- The **teaching deck** *How we (should) deliver*, for the Business Value Ladder — the cleanest way to present understanding back as *why · what · how*.
- Sit in on someone else's Confirm Scope before running one. This discipline is learned by watching more than by reading.

**Externally**

- Sam Kaner, *Facilitator's Guide to Participatory Decision-Making* — the standard work on running a session that has to produce a decision rather than a discussion.
- Gojko Adzic, *Impact Mapping* — a compact method for showing the line from business goal to deliverable, which is exactly what part two of this session must do.
- Chip and Dan Heath, *Made to Stick* — on presenting an understanding so it survives the room. Short and directly useful for the first part.

## Common failure

**Running it as a discovery workshop.** If the session opens the scope rather than closing it, the scoping track has not finished and this session is being used to hide that.

**Presenting the solution without the understanding.** Skipping straight to what we will build saves twenty minutes and loses the trust the first part was there to earn.

**No decision in the room.** The session ends with "we will come back to you" because the people who could decide were not there. That is a preparation failure, not a customer failure.

**Leaving the exclusions implicit.** Everything not mentioned is assumed included. That assumption is discovered in Development, at our cost.

**Confirming verbally and never writing it down.** Six months later there are two versions of what was agreed, and ours is the one nobody signed.

## Artefacts

The confirmation material itself — presented, then sent. Feeds **A100** where the scoping track produces one, and the project description. The written record of what was confirmed, what was excluded, and what remains open is an input to the contract and to the **Expectation matching** discipline that follows.

## How it varies by approach

- **Scoping & Planning** — its natural home. The session closes the workshop track and hands over to the decision about what to buy.
- **Analysis** — the most formal version. Scope is being locked so a price can be fixed, so the exclusions and the open list carry contractual weight.
- **Discovery** — a lighter version, and a different second part. Nothing is being locked, so what is confirmed is the *direction* and the MVP use case, not a scope. Saying that clearly is the whole job.
- **Onboarding/handover** — confirms what exists and what we are taking on, rather than what will be built.
- **Prototyping first** — this session is where the prototype's findings become a scope, and where the boundary between "what we showed you" and "what we will build" must be stated most explicitly.

**Traditional vs agentic.** The session is human either way. What changes is preparation: the material can be drafted from workshop notes, transcripts and the prototype rather than written from scratch, which frees the time that should go into rehearsing the difficult part of the conversation rather than into building slides.
