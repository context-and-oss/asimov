# Status reporting

*A **cross discipline**, from the first week to the closure report.*

## Purpose

Keep the customer's picture of where the work stands accurate: progress, hours spent and remaining, what has changed, what is at risk, and what happens next. It is the mechanism by which expectation matching actually happens, and it doubles as the paper trail that protects both sides when memories diverge.

## Why it matters

Six reasons, and the last one is usually left unsaid:

- **Alignment and transparency.** The customer always wants to know where things stand.
- **Progress.** What has been done, and what is next.
- **Hours spent and remaining.** The number that decides whether anyone is surprised at the end.
- **Decisions.** Changes need a place to surface and be accepted.
- **Risk.** A weekly look at the risk log is what keeps it alive.
- **Documentation.** The teaching deck calls this *"cover my ass"*, and being honest about that is better than pretending. A paper trail is what lets us show that an overrun came from clarifications that never arrived.

The old delivery model is blunt about the weekly update on time, budget and scope: it is mandatory, and *"it is MUCH better to communicate more than less."* A project can succeed even when it exceeds time, budget and scope, provided contact stayed close and honest throughout. The inverse is more common and more damaging: a delivery that met its specification, remembered as a failure because nobody was told what was happening.

There is a second benefit that has nothing to do with the reader. **Producing the report forces the discipline.** Writing the summary means taking stock of features, plan, outstanding items, change requests and risks, whether or not anyone reads it afterwards.

## How it is done

**Report weekly on time, budget and scope.** By email or a documented meeting. Not when there is news, but every week, because the weeks without news are what make the weeks with news believable.

**Cover seven things**, at whatever depth the engagement warrants:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Overview** | Objectives, scope, current status, kept brief |
| **Milestones and deliverables** | Including any change to deadline or scope |
| **Progress and performance** | Against the plan, with numbers and judgement |
| **Budget** | Spent, remaining, and the variance explained |
| **Resources** | Usage, availability, and any staffing problem |
| **Issues and risks** | What is impacting the work and what is being done |
| **Upcoming** | What happens in the next period |

**Use a red / amber / green summary.** It is a tool for the reader's manager: red means *I have to get involved*, amber means *challenges, but the team expects to hold it*, green means *under control*. A report where everything is always green is not a report.

**Prepare it in advance and review it at the meeting**, so corrections are made in front of the customer rather than after.

**Adapt the form to the task.** The full report is not needed on every engagement. Email, a shared note, a document: whatever works. What is not negotiable is that a continuous paper trail exists. The summary alone carries most of the value.

## What you can do at each level

| Level | What you can do |
|---|---|
| **Do it** | You can produce the weekly update on time, budget and scope reliably, and fill in a status report from the project's own data |
| **Show it** | You can write a status that says where things actually stand, including the amber, and explain a variance rather than presenting it. You choose the right form for the engagement instead of defaulting to the full template |
| **Build it** | You can set up an engagement's reporting so it is cheap to produce and honest by construction, because the data comes from where the work happens rather than being assembled by hand. You use the report to drive decisions, not to record them |
| **Own it** | You can report a project in trouble to a customer in a way that keeps the relationship and gets the decision you need. You set what good reporting looks like across engagements and coach people out of green-washing |

## How to get better

**Internally**

- The **teaching deck** *How we (should) deliver*. Its reporting section is the most complete material we have: why, what, how, and the exercise that goes with it.
- The old delivery model's **project management** page and its *"Bare Minimum"* principle: always communicate spend against budget per sprint, even when the customer says they do not need formal reports.
- Read a finished engagement's reports end to end. Where the trouble first became visible, and how long before it was named, is the most instructive thing you can learn from.

**Externally**

- Andy Grove, *High Output Management*. His point that a report's main value is in **writing** it, not reading it, is exactly what the teaching deck observes and worth having stated well.
- Scott Berkun, *Making Things Happen*. Practical chapters on project communication, and honest about the politics of reporting bad news.
- Edward Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, for showing a burn-down or a variance so it tells the truth at a glance rather than needing a paragraph of explanation.

## Common failure

**Reporting activity instead of position.** A list of what the team did is not a statement of where the project stands.

**Green until it is red.** Status that jumps from fine to crisis with nothing in between means the amber was being managed rather than reported.

**Writing it for the file.** A report produced because the template exists, read by nobody, is pure cost.

**Skipping it when things are bad.** The weeks it is hardest to write are the weeks it matters. A silence is read, and it is read correctly.

**Letting the customer opt out.** They may not want a formal report. They still need to know what has been spent, and the Bare Minimum principle exists for exactly this.

## Artefacts

The weekly update · the status report *(P300 in the old numbering; P400 for the email form)* · the risk log · the issue log · the change log · the project-status report from the delivery tooling, which needs a well-structured backlog to be worth anything.

## Where it is exercised

| Phase | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Proposal** | Little formal reporting, but the same discipline applies to the clarification track |
| **Scoping** | Progress against the workshop plan, and early visibility of what is turning out bigger than assumed |
| **Development** | Its main home: weekly progress meeting, monthly steering committee, and the report that feeds both |
| **Operations** | Becomes the service review: same questions, different cadence |

**By approach:** Scrum has the progress meeting built in alongside the ceremonies, and dropping the management cadence is the classic way to lose this. Kanban reports flow (throughput, cycle time, work item age) rather than hours, which is a different conversation the customer must have agreed to. Use-case delivery reports value delivered per use case, with the quarterly business demo as the senior-level version.

**Traditional vs agentic.** The content is unchanged. What changes is that most of it can be assembled from the tooling rather than typed, which removes the excuse for skipping it. It also moves the effort to the part that was always the point, the judgement about whether this is green, amber or red.
