# Cadence and meeting structure

*A **cross discipline**. Agreed at the start of an engagement and exercised throughout.*

## Purpose

Establish the rhythm the engagement runs on: which meetings exist, how often, who attends, and — the part most often left implicit — **what each one produces**. Set deliberately at the start, it is the skeleton everything else hangs on. Left to form by itself, it becomes a calendar nobody can defend.

## Why it matters

The meeting structure is where two things get decided that nothing else can fix.

**Where decisions happen.** If there is no meeting whose output is a decision, decisions happen in corridors and are remembered differently by everyone present.

**What the engagement costs in attention.** Meetings are the largest recurring cost on both sides, and the customer's senior people are the most expensive attendees in the room. A structure nobody designed will over-invite, over-run and under-produce.

There is a specific failure this discipline exists to prevent, and the old delivery model is explicit about it: **the Scrum meetings and the project management meetings do different jobs.** Scrum meetings are about development activities. Progress and steering meetings are about scope, time and budget. They complete each other, and running only the first is the most common way a delivery surprises its customer.

## How it is done

**Decide the structure at the start, with the customer's project manager**, not announced to them. It goes in the Scoping output and is part of onboarding the customer.

**For each meeting, settle four things: frequency, purpose, participants, and output.** A meeting without a defined output is a habit.

The old model's standard structure — the upper half from Scrum, the lower half traditional management — is a good default:

| Meeting | Frequency | Purpose | Participants |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Refinement** | Weekly | Prepare the next sprint and capacity; make sure the items are ready | Customer's product owner and our solution-responsible |
| **Sprint planning 1** | Per sprint | Talk through the items and resolve confusion | Customer's product owner and our team |
| **Sprint planning 2** | Per sprint | Re-estimate and distribute the items; balance against the sprint's capacity | Our team only |
| **Daily stand-up** | Daily | Coordinate progress and surface challenges | Both teams |
| **Demo** | Per sprint | Walk through the sprint's items and set up testing | Both teams |
| **Retrospective** | Per sprint | Evaluate the sprint; pick **one or two** improvements to try next sprint | Both teams |
| **Test** | Per sprint | The customer tests completed items | The customer |
| **Project plan review** | Monthly | Look at the remaining backlog from above: dependencies, challenges, risks. Output is an updated plan and risk picture | Solution-responsible and project managers, both sides |
| **Progress meeting** | Weekly | Status: scope, changes, time spent | Project managers, ideally with solution-responsible and the product owner |
| **Steering committee** | Monthly | Decide the big things; escalate risks to where they can be helped | Steering committee, project managers, solution-responsible, sponsor |

**Adapt it, then say what you adapted.** Not every engagement needs ten meetings. Removing one is fine; removing one without noticing what it produced is not — the output has to land somewhere else.

**Protect the two ends.** The daily coordination and the monthly steering are the two most often dropped, and they are the two hardest to reconstruct once gone.

**Review the structure when the engagement changes.** A cadence that fitted the build phase is usually wrong for hyper care and definitely wrong for operations.

## What you can do at each level

| Level | What you can do |
|---|---|
| **Do it** | You can run the agreed meetings on time, with an agenda, and produce what each is supposed to produce |
| **Show it** | You can set up a structure for an engagement from the standard, adapt it to the customer's reality, and explain to them why each meeting exists |
| **Build it** | You can design a cadence that fits the approach and the customer's decision speed — and remove meetings without losing their output. You notice when the structure has stopped fitting and change it |
| **Own it** | You can hold a cadence under pressure, including keeping the steering meeting when everyone would rather skip it. You set what a well-run engagement rhythm looks like and coach others to design one rather than inherit one |

## How to get better

**Internally**

- The old delivery model's **meeting structure** page — the fullest single page in the whole deck, and the table above is a condensation of it.
- The **project management** page, on why Scrum meetings and management meetings are not substitutes for each other.
- The **Workshop facilitation** discipline. Every meeting in the table is a session, and the same before/during/after applies at a smaller scale.

**Externally**

- Priya Parker, *The Art of Gathering* — on deciding what a gathering is for before deciding anything else, which is the whole of this discipline in one idea.
- Patrick Lencioni, *Death by Meeting* — specifically the argument that different meetings need different formats, and that mixing them is why they fail.
- The **Scrum Guide** — short, free, and worth reading in the original rather than as remembered. Particularly the parts about what each event is *for*.

## Common failure

**Inheriting the calendar.** The structure from the last engagement is copied without asking whether it fits this one.

**Only the Scrum half.** The ceremonies run, the progress and steering meetings quietly lapse, and the customer's first real news about budget arrives late.

**Meetings without an output.** Recurring, well-attended, and producing nothing that could not have been an email.

**Over-inviting.** Senior people in meetings that do not need them is expensive and teaches them to decline the ones that do.

**Never reviewing it.** The cadence set at kick-off is still running in hyper care, where it fits nobody.

## Artefacts

The agreed meeting structure, part of the Scoping output and of customer onboarding · agendas and meeting notes · the progress report from the weekly meeting · steering committee material. It is a section of the project description rather than a document of its own.

## Where it is exercised

| Phase | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Proposal** | Light: the clarification meetings and the handover meeting |
| **Scoping** | The workshop track has its own rhythm — and the structure for Development is *decided* here |
| **Development** | Its home. The full structure runs |
| **Operations** | Reduces to a service review and an escalation route |

**By approach:** Scrum brings the ceremonies with it and needs the management cadence kept alongside. Kanban replaces sprint events with replenishment, a daily flow check and a monthly service delivery review. Use-case delivery adds the quarterly business demo. Prototyping needs only one thing but needs it badly — a review with people who can decide, within days.

**Traditional vs agentic.** The structure is unchanged. What changes is the pressure on it: when work is produced several times a week, a weekly decision rhythm becomes the constraint. That is why each approach now states **what it requires of the customer** in cadence, and why that requirement is agreed in Scoping rather than discovered later.
