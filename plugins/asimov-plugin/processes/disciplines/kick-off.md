# Kick-off

*A **Development** discipline. Scoping has its own kick-off, and the same practice applies.*

## Purpose

Bring the project team together — the customer's people and ours — and establish a shared minimum understanding before the work starts. Goal, success criteria, who everyone is, what is being built, the plan in outline, and what could go wrong.

## Why it matters

A team that starts without a common picture spends its first weeks discovering the differences one at a time, usually at the point where each difference causes a problem. The kick-off is cheap and the alternative is not.

It also sets the tone. It is the first time both sides are in a room as one team rather than as supplier and customer, and how that session runs establishes what the working relationship will feel like.

And it produces the first risk list — which is the only reliable way to get one, because a risk list that is not started at kick-off tends never to be started at all.

## How it is done

**Gather everyone**: the customer's people and ours. Not the project managers alone.

**Cover six things.** Introduction to and information about the customer, so our people know who they are working for. The project goal and its success criteria. Introductions — actual ones, so people know who to ask about what. The solution in outline. The high-level plan. And the first version of the risk list, produced in the room.

**Add the practical.** Where things are, how to reach whom, which tools, what the meeting rhythm will be. Dull and worth ten minutes.

**Use what exists.** Previous projects' kick-offs are the fastest way to prepare one, and the slide template exists to be used — or the customer's, when that matters to them.

**Do not run it as a presentation.** The point is that everybody in the room ends up holding the same picture, and that requires them to talk.

## What you can do at each level

| Level | What you can do |
|---|---|
| **Do it** | You can prepare and run a kick-off covering the six areas, and produce a first risk list from it |
| **Show it** | You can run it so both teams actually meet — introductions that matter, questions surfaced, and a risk list the room contributed to rather than watched |
| **Build it** | You can design the session for the engagement: what needs establishing here, who must be present, and what the session should leave people believing |
| **Own it** | You can start an engagement that begins under strain — a difficult history, a sceptical team — and turn the room around in a session |

## How to get better

**Internally**

- The old delivery model's **Kick-off** page and the examples from previous projects it points to.
- The **workshop facilitation** discipline. A kick-off is a workshop, and the before/during/after applies in full.
- The **onboarding the team** and **onboarding the customer** disciplines, which are its internal and external companions.

**Externally**

- Diana Larsen and Ainsley Nies, *Liftoff: Start and Sustain Successful Agile Teams* — written specifically about this: chartering a team at the start. The most directly relevant book on the list.
- Priya Parker, *The Art of Gathering* — on what a beginning does, and on why most openings are wasted on logistics.

## Common failure

**Only the leads attend.** The people who will do the work never meet the people they will do it for.

**A status meeting in disguise.** The plan is presented, nobody speaks, and everyone leaves with the picture they arrived with.

**No risk list.** The single most concrete output, skipped for time.

**Held too late.** Work has already started, so the session ratifies decisions rather than shaping them.

**No practical information.** Three weeks of people not knowing who to ask.

## Artefacts

Kick-off material · the first version of the risk list · a note of what was agreed. It marks the point at which the meeting structure starts running.

## How it varies by approach

Scrum and Analysis-driven builds get a full kick-off. Use-case delivery kicks off the engagement once and then treats each new use case more lightly. Kanban's equivalent is agreeing the Definition of Workflow, which is the same act with a different name. Prototyping barely needs one — a short session on the question being answered is enough, and more would be ceremony.

**Traditional vs agentic.** Unchanged, with one addition worth making explicit in the room: **what the zone and level decision means in practice** for how this team will work, and what the customer has agreed to. Better said once at kick-off than discovered by someone three weeks in.
