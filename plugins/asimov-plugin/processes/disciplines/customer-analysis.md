# Customer analysis

*A **Scoping** discipline, starting from what the Proposal phase handed over.*

## Purpose

Make up our own minds about the customer: who matters, how they work, and what their situation allows. It is not a document produced for the customer — it is our own assessment, written down so that decisions about approach, cadence and risk have something behind them.

**It is not an analysis of the end users.** Those are examined in stakeholder analysis and in adoption. This is about the organisation we are contracting with.

## Why it matters

Every later choice assumes something about the customer. The approach assumes a decision speed. The estimate assumes a level of clarification support. The contract assumes a tolerance for uncertainty. Left unexamined, those assumptions are made anyway — just invisibly, and usually optimistically.

Writing it down also makes the assessment transferable. When someone new joins the engagement, a considered view of the customer is one of the most valuable things you can hand them, and it is almost never written.

## How it is done

Start from the handover material from Proposal, then look at three things.

**Stakeholders.** Who the important ones are on this project — feeding, and fed by, the stakeholder analysis.

**Maturity.** Agile or waterfall by habit; how much experience they have running IT projects; whether they know what a product owner actually does. This is what decides whether an approach that assumes fast decisions is realistic.

**Finances.** Whether more funding could be found if needed, and how exposed they are to an optimistic estimate or a run of change requests. A customer with no headroom needs a different shape of engagement than one with a contingency.

The list is not exhaustive, and it should not be treated as a form. Anything that would change how we run the engagement belongs in it.

**Write it as a judgement, not a description.** "They are a public-sector organisation" is a fact. "Their procurement rules mean any change over 100k needs a two-month approval, so our change process has to run ahead of the work" is an analysis.

## What you can do at each level

| Level | What you can do |
|---|---|
| **Do it** | You can complete the analysis from the handover material and what you observe, covering stakeholders, maturity and finances |
| **Show it** | You can turn observation into judgement — say what a customer's maturity *means* for how we should run the engagement, not just what it is |
| **Build it** | You can use the analysis to shape the proposal: approach, cadence, contract type and where to put the contingency. You revisit it when the customer surprises you |
| **Own it** | You can read a customer accurately early, including the parts they are not saying, and be right often enough that others check their read against yours |

## How to get better

**Internally**

- The old delivery model's **Customer analysis** page — one of its better-written, and the source of the three focus areas.
- The **change readiness** discipline, which asks a narrower version of the same question with a sharper instrument.
- Compare an early customer analysis with how the engagement actually went. The things you did not see are the lesson.

**Externally**

- David Maister, Charles Green and Robert Galford, *The Trusted Advisor* — on reading a client relationship, and on the difference between being useful and being trusted.
- Peter Block, *Flawless Consulting* — the chapters on discovery, and on the difference between the presenting problem and the real one.

## Common failure

**Describing instead of judging.** A page of facts about the customer that changes nothing about how we work.

**Doing it once.** Customers reorganise, sponsors leave, budgets get cut. An analysis from month one read in month eight is history.

**Only the flattering version.** An analysis that avoids saying the customer is inexperienced or under-funded protects nobody.

**Confusing it with the end-user analysis.** Different question, different phase, different discipline.

## Artefacts

The customer analysis, using the template where one exists. Feeds the **selection guide** decision about approach, the **estimate**'s assumptions, the **risk log**, and the shape of the contract proposed.

## How it varies by approach

It matters most where the approach demands something of the customer. Discovery and use-case delivery assume maturity and a real product owner; Kanban assumes someone who can prioritise weekly. If the analysis says those people do not exist, the approach has to change — that is what this discipline is for.

**Traditional vs agentic** makes no difference to the method. It raises the stakes on one finding: a customer who cannot decide quickly constrains an agentic delivery far more than a traditional one, because there is more waiting to do.
