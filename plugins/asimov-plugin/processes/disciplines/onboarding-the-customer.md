# Onboarding the customer

*A **Development** discipline, ideally attached to the kick-off.*

## Purpose

Give the customer an understanding of how we run projects, so they feel equipped to work with us. What the meetings are, how documents and approvals work, what our way of working means for them, how changes and open questions are handled, and which tools they will touch.

## Why it matters

Every approach we offer requires something of the customer — a product owner, a prioritiser, decisions within a number of days, testers in a window. Those requirements are only met if someone explained them, in advance, to the people who have to meet them.

It is also where a lot of later friction is prevented. A customer who has never been told that a change costs something will experience the first change request as bureaucracy. One who has been told will experience it as fairness. Same process, entirely different relationship.

And it protects the customer from us. Our document model, our approval points and our agile assumptions are unfamiliar and mostly invisible. Not explaining them means the customer participates in a process they cannot see the shape of, and then gets blamed for stepping outside it.

## How it is done

**Decide the shape.** One session or several. It can sit inside the kick-off or follow it, and for a large customer it is usually more than one conversation.

**Cover the collaboration**: the meeting structure and what each meeting needs from them · the document model and what the approval points mean · what our agile way of working implies — that scope is settled progressively, and that this is a feature rather than a defect · how changes are handled, including that they are priced · how open questions and clarifications are tracked, and what happens when one goes unanswered.

**Introduce the tools** they will actually use, and only those. Where the backlog lives, where documents live, where issues are logged.

**State what we need from them, concretely.** Named people, availability, decision speed. This is where the approach's requirements — the *what it requires of the customer* section on every approach page — get delivered as a conversation rather than a clause.

**Include anything specific**: agreements made with their project manager, local conventions, how they want to be contacted.

**Repeat it for newcomers on their side.** Customer teams change too, and a new product owner who never had this conversation will work the way their previous supplier taught them.

## What you can do at each level

| Level | What you can do |
|---|---|
| **Do it** | You can run the onboarding conversation covering meetings, documents, changes, issues and tools |
| **Show it** | You can pitch it at the customer's actual maturity — more for a first-time customer, less for an experienced one — and state what we need from them clearly enough that they agree to it |
| **Build it** | You can design the onboarding so the customer's obligations under the chosen approach are understood and accepted before delivery starts, not discovered during it |
| **Own it** | You can onboard a customer who is sceptical of our way of working, or attached to a different one, and get genuine buy-in rather than compliance |

## How to get better

**Internally**

- The old delivery model's **Onboard customer** page — short, and it lists exactly what to cover.
- Every approach page's *what it requires of the customer* section. That is the content of this conversation.
- The **expectation matching** discipline. Onboarding is expectation matching before there is anything to have expectations about.

**Externally**

- The literature here is thin — this sits between consulting practice and service management, and is rarely treated on its own. The closest useful reading is Peter Block's *Flawless Consulting* on contracting: agreeing how we will work together is part of the contract, not preamble to it.
- Priya Parker, *The Art of Gathering*, on openings — the same argument applies to opening a working relationship.

## Common failure

**Skipping it for an experienced customer.** They are experienced at *someone else's* process.

**Explaining our process instead of their part in it.** They do not need our document model explained as a model; they need to know what they must approve and when.

**Doing it once.** Their project manager changes and nobody repeats it.

**Vague requirements.** "We will need input from the business" produces nothing. "A product owner available two hours a week, and approvals within two working days" produces a conversation and sometimes a negotiation — which is the point.

**Treating it as ours to dictate.** It is agreed with their project manager, not announced.

## Artefacts

Onboarding material, adapted from the standard · the agreed meeting structure · a written note of what we need from them, which the Scoping output should already contain.

## How it varies by approach

This is where an approach's demands become real, so it varies most of all the disciplines. Kanban needs a prioritiser who understands that the commitment is probabilistic. Use-case delivery needs a product owner empowered to stop a pilot. Scrum needs someone available for refinement weekly. Prototyping needs deciders who can react within days and understand that what they are shown is not a product.

**Traditional vs agentic.** One thing is genuinely new and must be explained rather than assumed: **what the zone agreement means for them** — what AI may access, what it does not, and why we asked. Customers who have not had this explained tend to discover it at the worst possible moment, and it is a trust conversation rather than a technical one.
