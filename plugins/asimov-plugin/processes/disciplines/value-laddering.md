# Value laddering

*A **cross discipline** with three touch points: the ladder is built in Scoping, used to prioritise in Development, and read as the scorecard in Operations.*

## Purpose

Build an explicit link between three things: **why** the idea creates value, **what** goals will improve the business, and **how** we will implement it. The Business Value Ladder is the instrument, and its point is the *link* — a clear why makes the solution explainable to users and stakeholders, and success criteria make value visible while work is still in progress rather than only at the end.

Business value is the **net benefit realised by the customer**, in monetary or non-monetary terms. There is no universal checklist of what counts; drawing it out is the work.

## Why it matters

Without a ladder, prioritisation runs on preference. Every feature request sounds reasonable in isolation, and the only available arguments are seniority and volume. With one, there is a sober question available: *which rung does this serve?*

It is also what makes value-based pricing defensible. If the price was set against an outcome, the ladder is the artefact that says what the outcome was — agreed at the time, not reconstructed afterwards.

And it changes the conversation with the customer. A customer who asks *"can you help migrate our documents to SharePoint?"* has already chosen a solution. The ladder turns the request back into the business outcome behind it, which is where our value actually is — and doing that visibly demonstrates that we understood their strategy rather than just their order.

**We do not own benefit realisation. The customer does.** What we own is that the ladder exists, is agreed, and can be referred back to — and we can help measure. That is an honest position, and it is stronger in a sales meeting than a promise nobody can keep.

## How it is done

**Build the three rungs.**

| Rung | Question | Content |
|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | WHY | How the idea creates value |
| **Our goals** | WHAT | Clear goals for improving the business |
| **Implementation** | HOW | How we implement, using best practice |

**Name the outcome category.** Most engagements claim all four and mean one:

| Category | Purposes |
|---|---|
| **Revenue** | Increase revenue — share of wallet, customer acquisition, new markets, products, segments · reduce the risk of revenue falling |
| **Costs** | Reduce costs — technology spend, headcount, time on non-selling work · reduce the risk of costs rising |
| **Compliance** | Legal exposure · regulatory compliance · investor confidence · business predictability |
| **Stakeholder satisfaction** | Brand perception · customer retention · competitive perception |

**Write success criteria that both business and IT recognise.** SMART, and tied to a business outcome rather than a delivery milestone. They do four things: secure funding for this and the next project, build trust with both sides, give scope control and expectation management, and create buy-in.

**Run the value loop.** Evaluate → prioritise → build → back into the roadmap, with the ladder at the centre. The ladder is not written once; it is the thing the loop turns around.

**Use it out loud.** A ladder in a folder changes nothing. It earns its keep when someone says *"that request does not serve any rung — should we change the ladder or drop the request?"*

## What you can do at each level

| Level | What you can do |
|---|---|
| **Do it** | You can build a value ladder with the customer from a defined outcome, name the category, and write success criteria that are actually measurable |
| **Show it** | You can run the session that produces it — including turning a solution request back into the business outcome behind it — and get the customer to agree criteria they will later be held to |
| **Build it** | You can use the ladder to steer: prioritisation arguments settled against it, scope decisions traced to it, the roadmap built from it. You keep it current as understanding improves |
| **Own it** | You can build a ladder at executive level where the value is contested, tie it credibly to a price, and return at the end to ask whether the value arrived — including when the answer is no |

## How to get better

**Internally**

- The **teaching deck** *How we (should) deliver* — the full method, the outcome categories, the value loop, a worked real-world example and an exercise. This is the source; start here.
- The **Outcome definition** discipline, which produces the top rung this instrument hangs from.
- The real-world example's structure is a usable template: vision · purpose · value of the solution · preconditions · success criteria.

**Externally**

- John Doerr, *Measure What Matters* — objectives and key results. Different vocabulary, same problem: turning an ambition into something measurable without turning it into a task list.
- Gojko Adzic, *Impact Mapping* — the visual method for drawing goal → actor → impact → deliverable, and for exposing the deliverables that connect to nothing.
- Robert Kaplan and David Norton on the **Balanced Scorecard** — the origin of thinking about outcomes in categories rather than as a single number, which is what the four categories above are doing.

## Common failure

**The ladder built once and filed.** It becomes a slide in the kick-off deck that nobody opens again, and prioritisation goes back to running on preference.

**Success criteria that measure delivery.** *"The solution is live"* is a milestone. *"80% of the target group uses it weekly within three months"* is a criterion.

**All four categories claimed.** An engagement that improves revenue, costs, compliance and satisfaction equally has not been thought about.

**Criteria nobody can measure.** If verifying it needs data the customer does not collect and will not start collecting, it is decoration — and everyone knows it, which costs credibility.

**Promising the benefit rather than the measurability.** We make value visible; the customer realises it. Blurring that is how an outcome-priced engagement turns into an argument.

**Never going back to it.** The ladder's last rung is read in Operations. Skipping that step means the whole instrument was decoration after all.

## Artefacts

The value ladder itself, with success criteria. Feeds **A100** and the business specification, the **feature roadmap** where one exists, and the closure conversation about whether value arrived. It is the reference the **selection guide** points at when a request has to be judged rather than accommodated.

## Where it is exercised

| Phase | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Proposal** | Sketched — enough to shape the offer and the price conversation |
| **Scoping** | Built properly, in a workshop, with the customer. Its home |
| **Development** | The prioritisation reference. *Which rung does this feature serve?* |
| **Operations** | The scorecard. Did the value arrive — and if it is too early to say, when can it be said and who will look |

**By approach:** use-case delivery depends on it most, because each use case is chosen against it and the validation gate needs something to test against. Discovery produces the first version of it. Scrum uses it to defend a backlog order. Kanban is the exception — a stream of maintenance work often ladders to a service level rather than to a business outcome, and forcing a ladder there produces fiction.

**Traditional vs agentic.** The method is unchanged. Its value goes up: when building is fast and cheap, the expensive mistake is building the wrong thing efficiently, and the ladder is the cheapest available defence against that.
